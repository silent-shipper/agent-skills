# Write Essay

Write like a person sitting at a keyboard figuring something out. Not like an AI producing an essay.

## The Voice

First-person. Conversational. You're talking to a friend who's smart but doesn't have your specific context. You meander a little. You contradict yourself sometimes. You get distracted by things that matter to you personally and that's fine — those digressions are what make it feel real.

Ground claims in specific numbers and names, but drop them naturally, not like you're citing sources. "Forty chains, one app, solvers racing to fill your trade, done in seconds" — not "the platform supports 40+ blockchain networks."

```
# YES — sounds like a person wrote it
Everyone who's been in DeFi long enough has their version of this story.
Mine involves losing track of wrapped ETH on a chain I forgot I was even
using. Not my proudest moment.

# NO — sounds like an AI wrote it
The DeFi ecosystem presents numerous challenges for cross-chain users,
including asset tracking across multiple networks.
```

## How It Should Feel

- **Like you're thinking out loud.** Sentences don't all do exactly one job. Some trail off. Some circle back. The paragraph structure isn't perfectly parallel.
- **Self-aware without performing self-awareness.** "I'm biased. I work here." is good. "I must acknowledge my potential conflict of interest" is robotic.
- **Generous to others.** Give credit to other projects genuinely. "I use both. I respect both. But I keep running into the same wall with them." Not "other approaches have significant limitations."
- **Specific about your experience.** "I toggled it on, did the same Arbitrum-to-Solana trade I'd done a hundred times" — not "users can easily enable the feature."
- **Honest about what you don't know.** "I don't think people have fully clocked it yet" is better than "this represents a paradigm shift."

## Structure (target: 1,500-2,500 words)

Don't follow a rigid template. But generally:

1. **Start with your experience.** Not a thesis. Not a provocation. Just — here's what happened to me. Let the reader into a specific moment. A swap you did. A product you tried. A problem you kept hitting.

2. **Give context by wandering into it.** "I want to explain why I think that matters, but to do that I need to talk about where privacy has been living in crypto." You're bringing the reader along, not presenting a structured argument.

3. **Be generous to the landscape.** Talk about other products and people with genuine respect. Use them. Name what they do well. Then explain where you keep hitting the wall. The criticism comes from lived experience, not from a comparison matrix.

4. **Build to the insight naturally.** Don't announce the thesis. Let it emerge from the accumulated frustration and experience. "That's what near.com is becoming and I don't think people have fully clocked it yet" — the reader arrives at the conclusion with you.

5. **End personally.** Not with a grand reframe. With something honest about your own relationship to the thing you built. "I'm biased. I work here. But I also use this thing every day."

## What Makes It Sound Human

- **Incomplete thoughts that still land.** "Not my proudest moment." "That tells you everything."
- **Ellipsis moments.** "most users just... didn't bother."
- **Sentences that start with conjunctions.** "And adoption started climbing." "But there was always this thing in the back of my mind."
- **Qualifying yourself casually.** "which is more than I can say for most products I've worked on"
- **Observations that feel like they just occurred to you.** "The privacy isn't a moment. It's a state." — this reads like a realization, not a prepared line.
- **Varied paragraph length.** Some paragraphs are five sentences. Some are one. Let the rhythm be uneven.

## What Makes It Sound Like AI (avoid these)

- Every paragraph opens with a topic sentence and closes with a conclusion
- Perfectly parallel structure ("X gives you A. Y gives you B. Z gives you C.")
- Hedging language ("it's worth noting", "it should be mentioned", "arguably")
- Announcing structure ("Let me explain", "There are three reasons", "First... Second... Third...")
- Mechanical transitions ("That said", "However", "Moreover", "Furthermore")
- Symmetrical sections that all do the same thing at the same length
- Summarizing what you just said at the end of a section

## Don'ts

- Don't lecture. You're figuring this out alongside the reader.
- Don't use bullet lists in the body. This is prose.
- Don't summarize at the start. No "In this essay I will argue..."
- Don't be balanced for balance's sake. If you think something is better, just say so.
- Don't end with a grand universal statement. End with something personal.

## Process

1. Start writing from a specific moment — something you did, saw, or used.
2. Follow the thought wherever it goes. Don't outline first.
3. When you notice you're being too structured, break the pattern. Add a digression. Say something slightly off-topic that reveals personality.
4. Read it back and cut anything that sounds like a report. If a sentence could appear in a press release, delete it.
5. The last line should feel like the end of a conversation, not the conclusion of an argument.

Use `---` between major sections. Short paragraphs. White space is punctuation.
